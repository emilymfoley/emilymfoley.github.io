Honors thesis in Physics

I worked with Paul Anderson to study semiclassical quantum effects due to a massless scalar field in full 4D Schwarzschild–de Sitter (SdS) spacetime, a solution to Einstein’s equations corresponding to an expanding universe with a black hole formed from collapse. This project fits within the broader framework of Hawking’s discovery that black holes emit thermal radiation due to effects from quantum fields in curved spacetime. The Unruh vacuum state corresponds to a radiating black hole and is related to the Unruh effect, where acceleration gives rise to a perceived thermal bath even in empty space. While previous studies examined these states in 2D SdS or in 4D Schwarzschild space, their behavior in full 4D SdS spacetime far away from the black hole remains poorly understood. In my work, I numerically computed a set of mode functions from a related vacuum state and used them to construct Unruh modes and analyze their late time behavior. Preliminary results suggest that radiation from the black hole is non negligible even in the cosmological region far from the black hole. Stay tuned for a publication coming soon!

Mathematics Senior Thesis

For my undergraduate thesis in mathematics, I studied the dynamics of the Sine Gordon Equation, a well known nonlinear hyperbolic PDE that models a classical wave coupled with simple pendulum motion. The aperiodic late time behavior of stationary solutions to the damped driven sine gordon equation were of particular interest to me. When damping and forcing are present, solutions exhibit aperiodic late time behavior that is sensitive to initial conditions, which are signatures of spatiotemporal chaos. To visualize regimes in phase space where the system exhibits chaotic behavior, I numerically computed millions of Lyapunov exponents, which quantify the separation rate of perturbed solutions as exponential functions.

REU at the University of Minnesota

Gravitational wave signals detected by LIGO are sometimes accompanied by optical counterpart signals known as kilonovae emitted from the original binary neutron star or neutron star-black hole merger. Kilonovae are the primary source of heavy element production in the universe, yet they are only bright enough to observe for a short time. It is therefore important to optimize valuable telescope time when searching for such signals by assessing their ability to detect and localize events. I worked with Michael Coughlin in my REU at the University of Minnesota where I generated “observing scenarios” to simulate the detection and localization of GW signals. Using an advanced population model, I created approximately 10,000 monte carlo simulated GW signals and simulated the brightness of their optical signals over time with the multidimensional monte carlo radiative transfer code POSSIS. I then used LIGO’s dedicated data grid for analyzing large volumes of GW data to ​​provide realistic predictions for detection rates, localization areas, and effectiveness of different instruments in tracking GW events.
